Reading the Scope

A field guide to every mark on the scope — what each colour, shape, ring, line and motion means, and how to read them together. The scope is a relationship map of the Wi-Fi around you: who talks to whom, how strong the signal is, how busy each device is, and where the security risks are.

01Anatomy of a contact

Every device Kismet hears is a “contact” — a dot on the scope. A single contact can carry several marks at once. Here is one fully-loaded access-point contact with every layer switched on:

One access-point contact with role colour, signal gauge, security ring, busy-glow and label.

start from the coloured core (what kind of device), then read the ring around it (signal), any extra rings (risk / alert), the halo (how busy), and the lines leaving it (who it talks to).

02Contact roles — the core colour

The solid centre of every contact is coloured by the kind of device Kismet thinks it is:

Access point

A Wi-Fi base station broadcasting a network. Usually the hubs of the scope.

Client

A device that has joined (or is looking for) a network — phones, laptops, IoT.

Bridged / wired

A device seen behind an AP — typically reached over Ethernet and bridged onto the Wi-Fi.

Ad-hoc

A peer-to-peer / IBSS device not using a normal access point.

Unknown role

A Wi-Fi device Kismet couldn’t yet classify as AP or client.

SSID network diamond

Not a device — the named network itself. APs advertise them; clients probe for them.

colour answers “what is this?”. Amber hubs are access points; teal leaves are clients; pink diamonds are the network names tying them together.

03Shape & size

Shape

Devices are circles. Network names (SSIDs) are diamonds, so you can pick the “networks” layer apart from the “devices” at a glance.

Size

A contact’s diameter grows with its number of links (its degree) — on a square-root scale, so a hub with 40 clients is bigger than a lonely device, but not 40× bigger.

bigger dot = more connected. The biggest dots are the busy hubs worth investigating first.

04Signal gauge — the arc around a contact

A thin arc hugs each contact that Kismet has a signal reading for. Its length (how far around the circle it sweeps) and its colour both encode the received signal strength in dBm — closer / stronger fills more of the ring and shifts warmer.

-95 dBm · far / weak-40 dBm · near / strong

a nearly-full, warm/red arc = a strong, nearby signal. A short, blue sliver = a faint, distant device. It’s a proximity gauge, not a data-rate.

05Phantom contacts — hollow dashed circles

Referenced, not captured

An AP tells us it has a client with a certain MAC, but Kismet never heard that client directly. It’s drawn as a hollow, dashed outline instead of a solid dot.

a dashed empty circle is a device we know exists (an AP vouches for it) but haven’t actually seen on the air. If it later transmits, it fills in as a normal solid contact in place.

06Security rings — a dashed, spinning ring

A slowly-rotating dashed ring appears only on networks with an encryption weakness. Well-configured networks (WPA2 / WPA3, no WPS) get no ring at all — so any ring you see is worth a look. The colour tells you the problem:

Open red

No encryption at all — anyone can join and all traffic is in the clear.

WEP orange

The old, cryptographically broken standard — trivially crackable.

WPS enabled amber

Even on an otherwise-fine WPA2 network — WPS’s PIN is a known brute-force attack vector.

Note

The rotation is purely decorative — it exists only to catch your eye. The meaning is the ring’s presence and colour, not its motion. Adjust or stop the spin with the Ring spin slider, or hide the layer entirely from the legend.

see a spinning ring → that network is weak. Red = wide open, orange = WEP, amber = WPS on. Hover or click it for the exact crypto string.

07Alerts — a solid red pulsing ring

Flagged by Kismet’s IDS

The device is named in one or more Kismet alerts — deauthentication floods, AP spoofing, and other anomalies. A red Alerts panel at the top-centre lists the recent ones; click a row to jump to the device, and open its dossier for the full alert text.

a solid red, pulsing ring (distinct from the dashed spinning security ring) means “Kismet raised an alarm about this device.” Check the Alerts panel for what and when.

08Activity — glow, flow & ping

Three marks show live traffic, all driven by each device’s recent packet rate:

Busy glow

A soft halo behind a contact that grows and brightens with how much traffic it’s moving. Chatty devices glow; idle beacon-only devices stay flat.

Traffic flow

Dashes stream along the links of notably-busy devices — a sense of where data is actually moving right now.

Live ping live only

A quick ripple blooms from a contact the moment new packets arrive for it (while running --live). Several per update on a busy network is normal.

glow = how busy overall; flowing links = where traffic is going; a ripple = something just happened here, this second. Hover a device for its exact rate (~N pkt/min).

09Activity fade — brightness by recency

Contacts dim toward the background the longer they’ve been silent, so the brightest dots are the ones active right now. Live, this tracks real time; on a saved capture it shades the oldest contacts down and the most-recent ones up across the capture’s span.

bright = seen recently, faint = gone quiet. In live mode you can watch devices fade out and light back up as they come and go.

10Links — the lines between contacts

Lines show relationships. Their style tells you the kind of relationship:

Association

A client is joined to an access point. The backbone of the graph.

SSID advertise

An access point broadcasting a named network (AP → SSID diamond).

Probe request

A client actively looking for a named network it remembers (client → SSID).

Same-AP radio

A short glowing amber link tying together several BSSIDs that belong to one physical radio (e.g. a multi-SSID router).

Traffic flow

Animated dashes over an association link whose device(s) are busy right now (see Activity).

solid teal-grey = “is connected to”; amber = “advertises”; dashed pink = “is asking for”; flowing dashes = “is busy”.

11The dossier — click any contact

Clicking a contact dims the rest of the scope, highlights its immediate neighbours, and opens a dossier panel with everything Kismet knows about it:

  • Security — the encryption string and whether WPS is on (flagged red if risky).
  • Radio — vendor, channel, band (2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz), channel width, TX power.
  • Signal — current dBm and the min–max range seen.
  • Traffic — total packets and data volume; associated-client and probed-SSID counts.
  • Signal graph & Packet graph — sparklines of the last minute of signal strength and packet rate, so you can see trends at a glance.
  • Alerts — full text of any IDS alerts naming this device.
  • Connected contacts — a clickable list of neighbours; that’s how you hop from device to device through the graph.

the scope is the overview; the dossier is the detail. Click to drill in, click a neighbour in the list to walk the network, press Esc or the ✕ to step back out.

12Controls & interaction

The legend (bottom-left) is also the control panel

Every visual in this guide has a row in the legend. Click a row to toggle that layer on or off — contact types (which filters those devices out), link kinds, the signal gauge, security & alert rings, activity fade, busy glow, live ping, the dossier graphs, and the radar backdrop.

Top-right panel

  • Search — by MAC, name, SSID, vendor, or encryption (e.g. open, wpa3); Enter jumps to the first match.
  • Spread / Link length — retune how the layout spaces things out.
  • Ring spin — how fast the security rings rotate; slide fully left for off.
  • Freeze — pin every contact in place (stops the layout drifting); Reset view re-fits everything.

Mouse

Hover for a quick readout · click to open the dossier · scroll to zoom · drag the background to pan · drag a contact to reposition it.

13Important caveats

Read these before drawing conclusions
  • This is a relationship map, not a geographic one. Positions come from a force-directed layout — proximity on screen means “related”, not “physically near”. (Geography needs a GPS source at capture time.)
  • Only associated clients appear under an AP. A client shows up once it has actually joined a network. Idle devices sending randomised probes won’t appear as clients — but if they probe for named networks, they link to those SSID diamonds.
  • One physical AP can appear as several contacts — each SSID gets its own BSSID. The glowing amber “same-AP radio” links tie them back together.
  • Motion is not data. The gentle drifting of contacts is the layout settling; the ring spin and glow pulse are decorative. Only the marks themselves (colour, size, ring presence, arc length, halo, flow, ping) carry meaning.
